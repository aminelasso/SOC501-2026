 # SOC501-2026
Course material, datasets, and scripts for SOC501 ("Sociology: Theory and Methods") - Fall 2026. 

### Course description

This course equips students with the foundational skills to embark on any career that involves the social sciences. It has two main objectives: 
1) Build the students' general knowledge in the social sciences. After this class, students should know the shared core principles of the social sciences and the diversity of its theoretical and methodological approaches.
2) Turn this knowledge into operational skills: generate relevant research questions in the social sciences; make explicit and evaluate the theoretical assumptions that underlie social science research; and learn how to position oneself and justify one's own assumptions.

This course is built upon the assumption that thinking like a social scientist is a skill in itself. It does not come naturally and requires practice. Moreover, it assumes that this set of skills is relevant in academia and beyond. Your ability to think like a creative social scientist is one of the key ways in which you will stand out in your professional career. For this reason, instead of reviewing social science paradigms and methods as a list to memorize, the course is built around a small number of foundational debates. Students learn to analyze, recognize and position themselves in the foundational debates of the social sciences. The course is highly applied. Every debate is explored through concrete social objects—such as racism, economic behavior, family structures, or the causes of economic development, as well as through research topics that students will themselves choose and work on throughout the semester. Instead of passively listening and memorizing concepts, students are expected to engage actively. Classes feature structured debates and frequent exercises where students apply what they’ve learned to solve real social science problems.


### Learning outcomes

Students will build both knowledge and skills. 

##### Knowledge

* Knowledge of the core scientific principles shared across the social sciences.

* Knowledge of the main theoretical paradigms and methods in the social sciences.

* Knowledge of the foundational debates in the social science.


##### Skills

_Generate relevant research questions in the social science_

* Formulate a good research question in the social sciences.

* Translate everyday discourse (media claims, political rhetoric, conversation) into social-science questions.

*  Apply "sociological wonder": turning everyday experiences into social science questions


_Make explicit and critically evaluate the assumptions that underlie social science research_

* Identify the theoretical and empirical presuppositions of an analysis, and their strengths and limits.

* Identify how differences between paradigms and methods constrain interdisciplinary work.

* Identify how theoretical assumptions influence methodological design.

* Communicate across disciplinary boundaries.

_Position yourself_

* Justify the use of a paradigm or method in front of an audience.

* Apply a paradigm or method to a specific social-science object.

### Pedagogical arrangement

_General outline_

Every class, except for the first, begins with a 5-to-10-minute quiz covering the previous session's material and mandatory readings. 

The first two classes introduce core social science concepts, with hands-on exercises designed to train students in formulating research questions and translating everyday observations into social science research. 
Following these foundations, ten classes are dedicated to five major social science debates, illustrated with concrete social objects. Each debate spans two sessions: a first session features a lecture introducing the topic with examples and exercises, while later on a second session is a workshop built around student presentations followed by collective deliberation, from which every student writes a short report. Finally, the last session is dedicated to students presenting their final essays.

### Assessment

Your grade comes from four assessments.

**Participation, Contribution & Engagement — 10%**

This assessment covers three main sources of grades, each worth a third. First, every class except the first opens with a short quiz on the previous week's lecture and the current week's readings. Second, students will regularly do in-class exercises, some of which are graded. Finally, students are expected to keep a weekly notebook, where they note things they've noticed and start turning them into possible social science research questions.

**Continuous Assessment — 20%**

This assessment covers the final essay and its oral component. Students write a research essay on a social science question of their choice, relevant to the Moroccan context. The goal is to formulate a precise research question, identify the different paradigms and methods that have been used to tackle it, and explain which they prefer and why. The essay has two components, each worth half the grade: an oral presentation given during one of the workshops, and the written essay itself.

**Midterm Exam — 30%**

Students analyze an article by identifying and discussing the theoretical assumptions and methodological decisions made by the author(s). In-class, pen-and-paper, two hours.

**Final Exam — 40%**

Similar to the midterm: analyzing an assigned text and answering conceptual questions, but covers the whole course. In-class, pen-and-paper, three hours.
